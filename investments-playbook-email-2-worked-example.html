Where the other 2.7% goes

Yesterday I said the yield in the listing is not the yield that reaches your account. That is easy to assert and worth nothing until someone shows the working, so here is the whole calculation on one apartment, every line named.

A one bedroom at AED 1,500,000, advertised at AED 105,000 a year. Nine hundred square feet. The listing calls it a seven percent yield, and the listing is not lying.

What the listing shows

Purchase price 1,500,000
Annual rent 105,000
Gross yield 7.0%

What the rent actually loses

Service charge — 900 sq ft at AED 18 −16,200
Management, vacancy allowance,
maintenance reserve, insurance		 −19,700
Net operating income 69,100

What the purchase actually costs

Price 1,500,000
DLD transfer fee, 4% +60,000
Agency commission, 2% plus VAT +31,500
Trustee office and admin +4,600
Total outlay 1,596,100

69,100 ÷ 1,596,100

7.0%  →  4.3%

Net rental yield

Nothing dishonest happened anywhere in that. The advertised number simply answered a different question from the one you were asking. Gross yield asks what the rent is relative to the price. You were asking what the building pays you relative to what it took to own it.

Three ways this still goes wrong

Service charges are not fixed. An owners association can raise them, and a tower with an ageing chiller or a disputed handover can lose you a full percentage point of net yield overnight.
Use the achieved rent, not the asking rent. Putting the asking rent into a net yield calculation quietly rebuilds the same optimism you just stripped out.
If you borrow, this is not the whole picture. Net yield on total outlay and cash-on-cash return move apart sharply under a mortgage. Look at both, or you will misjudge how much risk the debt is carrying.
