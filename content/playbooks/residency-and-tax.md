Every page on this question answers half of it. The Dubai brokerage pages print the fee schedule and the words *no income tax, no capital gains tax, no annual property tax*, and stop. The accountancy pages say a UK resident is taxable on worldwide income and that a treaty exists, and print no number at all. Nobody runs the rent through both.

Run it through both and the answer is worse than either page implies, for a reason that is in the treaty's own drafting.

## The short answer, and then the arithmetic

The United Arab Emirates charges no personal income tax on residential rent. That is true, it is the whole of what the brokerage pages are telling you, and **it is a statement about the UAE, not about your tax bill.**

Tax is not levied by one authority using one test. It is levied by several, using different tests, and **moving house changes only one of them**.

## The four questions, which have four different answers

**Residence** asks where you live for tax purposes. This is the one a move changes, and it is what people mean by tax free. It is usually a day count combined with a ties test.

**Source** asks where the income arises. Rent is taxed where the building is, in almost every system on earth, and that does not care where the landlord sleeps. A flat in Manchester generates UK-taxable rent whether its owner is in Manchester or Dubai. The reverse is the case this page is about, and it is the one nobody writes up.

**Situs** asks where an asset is located for death duties, and it is a different test from source. This is the one that catches people through funds rather than property, which [fund domicile](/playbooks/fund-domicile/) covers in full: a US-domiciled ETF holds US-situs assets for estate purposes regardless of where its owner lives, and the threshold before US estate tax applies to a non-resident is far lower than most people assume.

**Citizenship** asks whether your passport taxes you wherever you are. For most nationalities the answer is no. For Americans it is yes, and no amount of moving changes it.

## The treaty relieves you of nothing, and it is drafted correctly

Take the commonest case: a UK resident who buys a flat in Dubai. The two countries have a double taxation convention, signed in 2016 and in force, and it does exactly what such a convention is supposed to do.

**Article 6** deals with income from immovable property. Income derived by a resident of one state from immovable property **situated in the other Contracting State may be taxed in that other State**. Read that carefully: *may be taxed*, not *shall be taxable only*. It permits the UAE to tax the rent. It does not stop the UK from taxing it too.

**Article 21** is the article that is supposed to stop the double charge. UAE tax payable under UAE law on income from sources within the UAE **shall be allowed as a credit against any United Kingdom tax computed by reference to the same profits, income or chargeable gains**.

**Article 2** lists what counts as UAE tax for that purpose: **in the case of the United Arab Emirates: (i) the income tax; (ii) the corporate tax**.

Now put the three together. The UAE levies no personal income tax on residential rent, so the UAE tax payable on it is nil, so the credit against the UK charge is nil, so the UK charge stands in full.

**The zero that makes Dubai attractive is the same zero that makes the treaty empty.** A credit relief article can refund a tax that was paid. It cannot refund a tax that was never levied. There is nothing wrong with the drafting and nothing to negotiate; it is simply that relief from double taxation is worth nothing to someone who was only ever taxed once.

## What that costs, on the flat this site already priced

The [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page prices an illustrative one bedroom at AED 1,500,000, 900 square feet, renting at AED 105,000. After vacancy, service charge, management, maintenance and insurance the net operating income is **AED 69,127**; after the acquisition stack of AED 96,220 the net yield is **4.33%**.

That 4.33% is a pre-tax number for every owner who is tax resident somewhere that taxes worldwide income. Charged at the owner's marginal UK rate, as a top slice on someone who already has UK income in the band:

| Owner's position | Rate | Tax on AED 69,127 | Kept | Net yield | Points lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| UAE resident | 0% | 0 | 69,127 | 4.33% | 0.00 |
| UK basic rate | 20% | 13,825 | 55,302 | 3.46% | 0.87 |
| UK higher rate | 40% | 27,651 | 41,476 | 2.60% | 1.73 |
| UK additional rate | 45% | 31,107 | 38,020 | 2.38% | 1.95 |

The arithmetic is rate-based, so it needs no exchange rate and cannot go stale on one. Which band applies depends on your sterling income, which is your fact and not this page's.

## The line nobody prints is the largest line

The net rental yield page's finding is that running costs take **2.39 percentage points** of yield and the entire transaction stack, meaning the 4% registration fee, the agency commission, the trustees and the certificates, takes **0.28**, a ratio of 8.5 to 1, the reverse of where every buyer negotiates.

Set the tax beside it. For a higher rate UK owner the charge takes **1.73 percentage points**, which is **6.2 times the whole transaction stack**. And the comparison understates it, because the transaction stack is paid once and the tax is paid every year the flat is let.

The single largest recurring cost of owning a Dubai flat, for a large share of the people buying them, is a line that does not appear on any page selling them. It is not hidden. It is simply charged by a different government than the one the brochure is about.

## The mortgage makes it worse, not better

Interest on a loan against a residential letting is not deducted from the profit. Relief arrives instead as a reduction of the tax bill. **The reduction is the basic rate value (currently 20%) of the lower of** the finance costs, the property profits, and the owner's income over the personal allowance.

For a basic rate taxpayer that is roughly neutral. For a higher rate taxpayer it is a real cost: relief at 20% against a charge at 40% loses twenty pence in the pound of interest. On AED 50,000 of annual interest that is **AED 10,000 a year**, and it is charged on top of the tax in the table above, because the table's profit is computed before any interest at all.

The consequence is the one that surprises people: a sufficiently geared property can generate a tax bill larger than the cash it produces, because the tax is computed on a profit the interest has already spent. [Mortgage capacity](/playbooks/mortgage-capacity/) sets out what the Central Bank will lend; this is the reason to borrow less than that.

## The four year window, if you are moving the other way

There is one case where the answer changes completely, and it is timing-sensitive enough to be worth knowing before the move rather than after.

Someone who becomes UK tax resident, and who is **still within your first 4 years as a UK tax resident following at least a 10-year period as a non-UK tax resident**, can claim relief under the four year foreign income and gains regime. The regime covers **profits of an overseas property business**, which is what a let Dubai flat is.

It is not free. Claiming it forfeits the personal allowance for income tax and the annual exempt amount for capital gains, and the relieved foreign income still counts in working out adjusted net income. Whether it is worth claiming is arithmetic on the reader's own numbers, and it is arithmetic with a deadline attached: the window opens on the day UK residence starts and it does not reopen.

This is the fact that most changes what a Dubai-based owner should do in the year they move, and it is on no page that sells Dubai property.

## What leaving actually removes

One thing. Residence-based taxation on worldwide income by the country you left.

That is genuinely valuable and it is frequently the largest single item. It is also, for someone with assets in more than one country, a long way from the whole bill.

The clean way to hold this: **leaving ends a claim on you. It does not end a claim on your assets.** Every framework in this library that touches tax is downstream of that distinction.

## The departure year is the complicated one

The common assumption is that the move is a clean line: taxed there before, not taxed there after. Almost no system works that way.

Split-year treatment decides how the year of departure is divided. Exit charges in some countries tax unrealised gains as though you had sold on the way out. And the country's own definition of when residence ended is often not the date on the flight.

This is why the framework says do it before rather than after. The decisions available in advance, about when to sell, when to move, and in which order, are largely unavailable once the year has closed. [Capital gains and the move to Dubai](/playbooks/the-year-you-sell/) prices the other end of it: a gain realised while abroad is charged on the return to the UK if the absence lasts five years or less.

## The ties test is what people get wrong

Almost everyone knows there is a day count. Fewer know that clearing it is necessary rather than sufficient.

A home that remains available to you, a family who stayed, a business you still run, memberships and registrations: these are ties, and ties tests exist precisely to catch people who moved on paper. Someone spending under the threshold while keeping a house, a spouse and a company in the old country can still be resident there, and will find out at the least convenient moment.

## India writes the zero into the test itself

Section 6(7) of India's Income-tax Act, 2025 deems an Indian citizen resident in India for a tax year if he is not liable to tax in any other country and his income other than from foreign sources exceeds fifteen lakh rupees, so the UAE's zero income tax is one of the three conditions rather than a protection from them.

That is a different mechanism from the British one above, and it runs the other way. The United Kingdom case is the ordinary one: you are resident there already, the treaty is empty, the charge stands. India's rule can make you resident in the first place, and the absence of a Dubai tax is not incidental to it. It is written into it.

Start with which statute is in force, because the section numbers are worthless without it. India replaced its tax code. The Income-tax Act, 2025 provides that **it shall come into force on the 1st April, 2026**, and the Central Board of Direct Taxes states in its own transition guidance that **The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaces the Income-tax Act, 1961 with effect from 01.04.2026**. The year this page is being read in is governed by the new Act. A page answering this question with section numbers from the 1961 Act is citing a statute that no longer governs the year it is about, and a reader who checks those numbers against the Act in force will not find the rule below at all.

Under the new Act a resident's total income includes income which **accrues or arises to such person outside India in that year**. Dubai rent is exactly that. So the whole question is residence, and residence has a second test most people have never read.

The day counts are the familiar part: 182 days in India in the tax year, or 60 days in the year together with 365 across the four preceding ones. Clear both and you are not resident on the day count. Then comes section 6(7), which provides that irrespective of those sub-sections, **an individual shall be deemed to be resident in India for a tax year** if three things are all true of him. He **is a citizen of India**. He **is not liable to tax in any other country or territory due to his domicile, residence, or similar criteria**. And he **has total income exceeding fifteen lakh rupees during such tax year (other than the income from foreign sources)**.

Read the second condition against the first page of any Dubai brochure. The UAE levies no personal income tax, so an Indian citizen living in Dubai is not liable to tax there by reason of his residence, and the condition is satisfied by the very feature that brought him. The zero is not neutral and it is not merely useless to him, as it is to the British owner further up this page. It is one of the three things the deeming rule requires.

## The flat is invisible to the trigger and fully exposed to the charge

The threshold in the third condition is on income **other than the income from foreign sources**, and the Act defines income from foreign sources as **the income, which accrues or arises outside India**. Dubai rent accrues outside India, so it counts nothing toward the 1,500,000 rupees that decides whether the rule bites, and the whole of that same Dubai rent falls into Indian total income if it does.

| Source of income | Counts toward the 1,500,000 rupee test | Taxed if the test is failed |
|---|---|---|
| Indian rent, interest, dividends, Indian capital gains | Yes | Yes |
| Dubai rental income | No | Yes |

Both halves of that matter, and they point in opposite directions. Buying a larger Dubai portfolio does not move you one rupee closer to being deemed resident. It also does not make the consequence any smaller on the day something else moves you there, and the something else is ordinary: an Indian flat let out, a fixed deposit, a dividend, the gain on a single Indian share sale.

The rule is also aimed precisely at the people who got the day count right. Section 6(8) provides that sub-section (7) **shall not apply to an individual, who is resident in India for a tax year under sub-sections (2) to (6)**, so it reaches only someone who is *not* resident on the day tests. The standard advice on this subject is to keep the days down. Keeping the days down is what puts a person inside the only population section 6(7) can reach, and clearing the day count more comfortably does not help at all once the three conditions are met.

What this costs is Indian income tax at the owner's own rate on the rent, and **this page does not print that rate.** The rate schedule for the year in question belongs to the 2025 Act as amended by the Finance Act, 2026, and the deductions available against house property income belong to its own sections; this site prints a figure only once it has read the instrument that sets it, which is the treatment the [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page gives the developer's transfer fee. The three conditions above are the part the statute settles, and they are the part that decides whether the rate concerns you at all. Take the conditions to an Indian adviser; do not take a number off a brokerage page.

## What no income tax does not mean

It also does not mean residency arrives with the property. What a two million dirham purchase does and does not buy, and how that compares with the European routes people weigh it against, is [its own framework](/playbooks/golden-visa-vs-portugal-d7/).

It does not mean no tax administration. Corporate tax applies to some structures and activities, which matters for anyone considering [corporate versus personal ownership](/playbooks/corporate-vs-personal-ownership/). A residency certificate has to be applied for and maintained if it is to be relied on. Filing obligations in the country you left may continue for years.

None of which undoes the advantage. For someone who actually lives in the UAE the advantage is exactly as large as advertised, and this page does not dispute it. It means the advantage belongs to residence, not to the building, and a buyer who is not moving does not get it.
