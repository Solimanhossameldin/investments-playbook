Every page on this question says there are two tests. A loan to value cap that limits the loan against the property, a debt burden ratio that limits it against your income, and your capacity is the lower of the two.

That is the summary of a regulation nobody has opened. [Article 3 of the Central Bank's Regulations Regarding Mortgage Loans](https://rulebook.centralbank.ae/en/rulebook/article-3-important-ratios) sets seven things, and **three of them cap the size of the loan**. The third is a maximum financing amount expressed as a multiple of annual income, and it sits in the same article, two clauses below the one everybody quotes.

Whether it matters is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is below. So is the answer to the question a buyer actually has, which is not "what is the cap" but "what do I need to earn".

## The three caps

**Against the property.** The loan to value bands, as Article 3 drafts them.

| Borrower and property | Maximum loan to value |
|---|---|
| National, first home, at or below AED 5 million | 85% |
| National, first home, above AED 5 million | 75% |
| National, second or subsequent | 65% |
| Expatriate, first home, below AED 5 million | 80% |
| Expatriate, first home, above AED 5 million | 70% |
| Expatriate, second or subsequent | 60% |
| Anyone, bought off plan | 50% |

For an expatriate buying a first home the article allows a **maximum 80% of the value of the property**; on a second one it allows **60% of the value of the property, regardless of value**. The phrase "regardless of value" is doing real work: there is no price at which an investment property gets a better band.

The other phrase doing real work is "of the value of the property". The cap is drafted against the value, not against the price, and when the bank's valuer comes in below the agreed price it is the lower figure the loan is computed from while the registration fee stays on the contract. [When the bank values a property below the price](/playbooks/when-the-valuation-comes-in-low/) prices that gap, and it is larger than most buyers hold back for.

A detail worth having, because it is in the drafting rather than in any summary of it. The nationals' bands read "less or equal to AED 5 million" and the expatriates' read "less than AED 5 million". An expatriate buying at exactly AED 5,000,000 is in neither band as written. It is a seam, not a loophole, and the sensible thing is to price the purchase at the lower band and be pleasantly surprised.

**Against the monthly income.** The debt burden ratio, which Article 3 imports from the consumer lending regulation and states as **50 percent of gross salary and any regular income from a defined and specific source at any time**. Every committed payment counts against that half, not just the mortgage. And the ratio is assessed on commitments, not balances: a car loan with eleven months left counts at its full monthly payment, which is why clearing a small loan can buy more capacity than saving the same money toward the deposit.

**Against the annual income.** This is the one that is missing from the summaries. Article 3 sets a maximum financing amount: **Expatriates: up to 7 years annual income**, and eight for a national. It is a cap on the stock of debt, not on the flow of payments, and no deposit or term makes it go away.

**The maximum tenor of the mortgage loan is 25 years**, which is what turns a monthly payment back into a loan.

## Which of the two income caps actually binds

Both scale with income, so the answer does not depend on how much you earn. It depends on the rate, and the regulation sets that too: lenders are required to **stress test the loan at (2 to 4) percentage points above the current rate of interest on the loan**. The payment that has to fit inside the half of your income is the stressed one, not the advertised one.

Set the two income caps equal and solve. They coincide at a stressed rate of **5.18%**. Below it the seven-year multiple is the tighter cap; above it the debt burden ratio is. Because the regulation requires two to four points to be added to the contract rate, the stressed rate sits above 5.18% for any contract rate above roughly 1.2% to 3.2%, which is to say, in practice, always.

**So the income multiple is the most quoted cap and almost never the operative one.** The regulation's own stress test is what decides which of the regulation's own caps applies.

Here is the same point as the number a buyer can use. On the illustrative one bedroom at **AED 1,500,000** that the [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page prices, an expatriate first home at the full 80% band borrows AED 1,200,000. This is the income needed to get it, over 25 years, with no other debt at all.

| Stressed rate | Monthly payment | Income the ratio needs | Income the multiple needs | Which binds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 7,015 | 14,030 | 14,286 | the income multiple |
| 6% | 7,732 | 15,463 | 14,286 | the debt burden ratio |
| 7% | 8,481 | 16,963 | 14,286 | the debt burden ratio |
| 8% | 9,262 | 18,524 | 14,286 | the debt burden ratio |
| 9% | 10,070 | 20,141 | 14,286 | the debt burden ratio |

Read the swap in the last column. It happens once, between the first two rows, and it happens at a rate low enough that the second row is the realistic one.

At a stressed rate of 6%, an expatriate borrowing the full 80% band on a AED 1,500,000 Dubai apartment needs a monthly income of AED 15,463 and no other debt at all, because Article 3 allows half of income to service every commitment the borrower has.

Two things follow that no advertisement mentions. The income in that table is the income needed **with no car loan, no personal loan and no credit card commitment**, so every dirham of other monthly obligation raises it by two. And the table is the answer to "what salary do I need to buy a one bedroom", which is asked far more often than it is answered with arithmetic.

## The deposit is not the deposit

The loan to value cap is a percentage of the value of the property. The registration fee, the agency commission, the trustee's fee and the mortgage's own registration are not the property, and none of them can be borrowed.

On the same AED 1,500,000 flat, at the full 80%:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Deposit, being the fifth the loan does not cover | 300,000 |
| Buying: registration, agency, trustee, title deed, map, fees | 96,220 |
| Borrowing: mortgage registration, trustee, title deed, fees | 7,470 |
| **Cash required on the day** | **403,690** |

That is **26.91% of the price** against an advertised **20%**, a gap of **6.91 percentage points**, or **AED 103,690** the buyer has to find that the deposit figure never mentioned. Every line of it is traced to its instrument on the [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page, and the mortgage's own registration fee, a quarter of one per cent of the debt, is the only one of them that scales with how much you borrow rather than with what you pay.

The whole of that stack is what [transaction cost drag](/playbooks/transaction-cost-drag/) measures from the return side. Here it is measured from the side that stops the purchase happening at all.

## Off plan is a different number entirely

Article 3 is blunt about this: **the maximum LTV for mortgages on property being purchased off plans is 50% regardless of purpose, value, or category of purchaser**. No first-home band, no price bracket, no nationality.

On the same unit that means a loan of AED 750,000 and cash of **AED 852,565**, or **56.84%** of the price. A buyer reading a 20/60/20 payment plan as "20% down" is out by a factor of nearly three on the cash the purchase needs, because the plan describes what the developer wants and the regulation describes what a bank may lend. The two have never been the same document. What happens if the instalments and the mortgage do not meet is [the off-plan page](/playbooks/off-plan-irr/), and it is the most expensive branch in Dubai property.

## What the regulation sets for a non-resident, which is nothing

The Central Bank's regulation sets no loan to value cap on a buyer living outside the UAE: Article 3 bands the maximum loan by nationality status and by whether the property is a first home, an investment or off plan, and never by residency. The 50% that brokers quote is bank policy, and at 50% the cash required on the AED 1,500,000 Dubai apartment this page prices is AED 852,565, or 56.84% of the price.

The regulation says it in its own voice, twice, in the Introduction a summary never reaches. It describes institutions **providing mortgage loans to UAE nationals, GCC nationals and expatriates**, which is three nationality categories with no residency condition attached to any of them. And it states the one distinction it does draw: **These Regulations make a distinction between loans to owner occupiers of residential property and investors in residential property**. Owner occupier against investor. Not resident against non-resident.

So the half that is printed on almost every page answering this question is not a cap, and the pages quoting it usually concede as much in the clause itself. The figure arrives as *some banks only finance up to 50% for non-resident applicants*, in the same article that attributes 20% and 30% down to "UAE Mortgage Law" without naming it. Those two are the regulation: they are the expatriate first-home bands either side of AED 5 million, stated as deposits. The 50% is a credit committee. Two different kinds of number, set out as one, which is why the answers disagree with each other from page to page while each one sounds settled.

What follows from that is not a better figure. It is that the figure is the wrong thing to plan against. Here is the cash the same purchase needs across the whole span a buyer abroad is actually quoted, on the illustrative **AED 1,500,000** one bedroom, with the fee stack that does not change whatever the loan is.

| Loan to value offered | Loan | Cash required on the day | As a share of the price | Against an advertised deposit of |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80% | 1,200,000 | 403,690 | 26.91% | 20% |
| 70% | 1,050,000 | 553,315 | 36.89% | 30% |
| 60% | 900,000 | 702,940 | 46.86% | 40% |
| 50% | 750,000 | 852,565 | 56.84% | 50% |

The gap in the last two columns is between **6.84 and 6.91 percentage points** on every row, because the acquisition stack is a share of the price and barely moves with the loan. A buyer who has prepared the deposit has prepared for the wrong number by about AED 103,000, and that is true at the statutory band and at the policy half alike.

Three things are worth taking from the table rather than from the figure. The cash column is what a purchase is planned against, and it is knowable to the dirham before any bank has been approached. The loan to value in the first column is negotiable, because policy is: a borrower told 50% by one lender has been told the policy of one lender, and [the valuation](/playbooks/when-the-valuation-comes-in-low/) is a separate hazard sitting underneath whichever band is agreed. And the two income caps above this section still apply in full, assessed on income earned abroad: the seven-year multiple, the stress test, and the debt burden ratio that is nearly always the one that binds. Income in another currency against a loan in dirhams is the risk that arrives with them, and [the peg](/playbooks/currency-risk-and-the-peg/) is what that risk actually rests on.

Whether the unit can be bought at all by a foreign national is a prior question with its own answer, which is a register rather than a policy: [freehold areas and foreign ownership](/playbooks/freehold-areas-and-foreign-ownership/).

## What counts as income, and what does not

Three clauses in Article 3 narrow the top of the ratio, and each costs money.

**Rent is credited at ten twelfths.** For an investment property the lender must **make a deduction of at least two months' rental income from the DBR calculation**. On the illustrative unit's AED 105,000 rent that is AED 17,500 removed and **AED 87,500** counted. The deduction is a floor, so a lender may take more.

**The gratuity does not count.** **The use of 'End of Service Benefit' is not allowed** as a source of repayment. A buyer counting an accrued end of service payment as part of the deposit is fine; a buyer counting it as income is not.

**And the term is capped twice.** Twenty-five years is the outside limit, but where the loan runs past retirement the lender must satisfy itself that the balance can continue to be **serviced at a DBR of 50 percent of the borrower's post retirement income**. That is the regulation's actual age rule. The figures of 65 and 70 that circulate are lender policy sitting on top of it, and policy is arguable in a way that a regulation is not.

## What to do with this

Compute all three caps before viewing anything, at a stressed rate two to four points above whatever you are being quoted, and take the smallest. Then subtract the whole acquisition stack from your cash, not the deposit alone, and search below what is left.

When the loan to value cap is the one that binds, the bank is saying you are comfortable and the asset carries risk it will not fully fund. When the debt burden ratio binds, which per the table above is nearly always, it is saying the property is fine and you are the constraint. Those are different problems with different fixes, and only one of them is solved by finding a cheaper flat.

Article 4 of the same regulation entitles you to check the arithmetic rather than accept it: **Borrowers should be provided with information setting out the total cost of the loan during its lifetime**, with fees and charges "detailed in a separate schedule". Ask for the schedule. Then run [due diligence on the specific unit](/playbooks/due-diligence-before-an-offer/), and know before you start what the [exit](/playbooks/selling-well/) costs, because the early settlement charge on this loan is capped by the same Central Bank and most sellers discover it in the week they are trying to complete. If the rate rather than the property is what you end up wanting to change, [what it costs to move the loan to another bank](/playbooks/refinancing-a-dubai-mortgage/) is published to the dirham before anyone runs a credit check.

Every instrument cited here is listed, with what it sets and a link to the official text, in the [Dubai property law register](/dubai-property-law/).
