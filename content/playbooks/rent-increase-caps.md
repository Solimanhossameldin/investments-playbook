Every page on this question prints the same five rows and then one worked example covering one year. The table is the easy part. The part that decides whether a tenanted property is worth buying is what happens on the second renewal, and the third, because the tier is recalculated each year against a gap your own increase has just narrowed.

## What the decree actually says

Article 1 of Decree No. (43) of 2013 sets the maximum increase by how far the rent sits below the average rental value. These are the decree's own words, not a paraphrase of them.

| How far below the index | Maximum increase | The decree's wording |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 10% | 0% | up to ten percent (10%) less than the average rental value |
| 11% to 20% | 5% | eleven percent (11%) to twenty percent (20%) less |
| 21% to 30% | 10% | twenty-one percent (21%) to thirty percent (30%) less |
| 31% to 40% | 15% | thirty-one percent (31%) to forty percent (40%) less |
| More than 40% | 20% | more than forty percent (40%) less |

So the permitted range runs from no increase at all up to a maximum of twenty percent, and which rung you are on is not a matter of opinion. Article 3 names the authority: the average rental value is determined by the Rent Index of the Emirate of Dubai approved by the Real Estate Regulatory Agency. Not the landlord's view of market rent, not the agent's, and not the rent the neighbour is paying.

## The Smart Rental Index changed the number, not the rule

The Land Department's Smart Rental Index was launched on 2 January 2025. It rates buildings on their technical and structural characteristics, the quality of finishes and maintenance, location, and the services and facilities available, and it revalues continuously rather than once a year.

**It did not change the tiers.** Decree 43 still sets what you may add. What changed is the number you measure the gap against, and it changed in a way that matters: the comparable set is now the building, so two towers on the same street can return different averages, and an argument from what the neighbour pays is weaker than it used to be.

## Notice, which is where landlords lose

Changing any term of a tenancy, rent included, requires the other party to be notified **no less than ninety days before the date on which the lease contract expires**, under Article 14 of Law No. (26) of 2007 as amended by Law No. (33) of 2008, unless the parties have agreed otherwise. Miss it and the tenancy renews on the existing terms.

Ending a tenancy on expiry because the owner wants to sell or occupy is a different provision. Article 25(2) requires the tenant to be notified of the reasons **at least twelve months before the date of eviction**, and the notice must be **served through a Notary Public or by registered mail**.

These are procedural, which is exactly why they decide disputes: a landlord with a perfectly good case loses it on service.

## The part nobody models: four renewals, not one

Take a unit whose index average is AED 120,000 and whose sitting tenant pays AED 78,000. That is 35% below, so the first renewal permits 15%. Then the arithmetic turns on itself.

| Renewal | Index | Rent | Gap | Permitted |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 120,000 | 78,000 | 35.0% | 15% |
| 2 | 120,000 | 89,700 | 25.3% | 10% |
| 3 | 120,000 | 98,670 | 17.8% | 5% |
| 4 | 120,000 | 103,604 | 13.7% | 5% |
| 5 | 120,000 | 108,784 | 9.3% | 0% |
| 6 | 120,000 | 108,784 | 9.3% | 0% |

**It stalls in year 5, at AED 108,784, permanently 9.3% below the index.** Not because anyone refused, but because raising the rent is what moved it into the band where no increase is permitted. Every starting gap does this. From 50% below it takes five renewals and stops about 8% short; from 15% below it takes two and stops about 6% short. **The ladder always terminates inside the bottom band, and it never terminates at the index.**

## So what is vacant possession actually worth

That is the same question asked in money. Against the identical unit bought empty and let at the index from day one:

| Year | Shortfall, AED |
|---|---|
| 1 | 42,000 |
| 2 | 30,300 |
| 3 | 21,330 |
| 4 | 16,396 |
| 5 | 11,216 |
| **Five year total** | **121,242** |

And it does not end in year five. The rent is frozen 9.3% below the index, so the tenanted unit keeps giving up about **AED 11,216 a year, indefinitely**, under a flat index.

A buyer choosing between this unit tenanted and the one next door vacant now has a number instead of an instinct. If the vacant unit costs less than about AED 121,000 more, before any view on what happens after year five, it is the cheaper purchase. That comparison belongs in the [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) calculation, not beside it, and the first year figure is the one that goes into [break-even occupancy](/playbooks/break-even-occupancy/).

## A rising index makes it worse, not better

The obvious hope is that a rising market rescues you, because the cap is generous when the gap is wide. It does the opposite. Hold the same unit and let the index rise 5% a year:

| Renewal | Index | Rent | Gap | Permitted |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 120,000 | 78,000 | 35.0% | 15% |
| 2 | 126,000 | 89,700 | 28.8% | 10% |
| 3 | 132,300 | 98,670 | 25.4% | 10% |
| 4 | 138,915 | 108,537 | 21.9% | 10% |
| 5 | 145,861 | 119,391 | 18.1% | 5% |
| 6 | 153,154 | 125,361 | 18.1% | 5% |
| 7 | 160,811 | 131,629 | 18.1% | 5% |

**The gap stops closing at 18.1% and stays there.** Once it falls inside the 11 to 20% band, the 5% you are permitted is exactly the 5% the index is gaining, so rent and index rise together forever at the distance they happened to be when you arrived. The cap is a speed limit on catching up, and above a certain market growth rate the speed limit is the market's own speed.

This is the honest version of a sentence you will hear from an agent: that a below-market tenancy is fine because rents are rising. Rising rents are precisely what freeze the gap.

## The tenant's side of the same rule

The rule is symmetrical in usefulness. A tenant facing an increase can run the Land Department's own calculator, and anything above the permitted figure can be declined without argument, because the figure is not a negotiating position. The Rental Dispute Centre exists for the disagreement that follows.
