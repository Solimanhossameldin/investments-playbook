Yield tells you what a property earns when everything goes right. Break-even occupancy tells you how much has to go wrong before it costs you money, and it is the more useful number because vacancy is the risk that actually happens. Prices fall occasionally. Tenants leave routinely.

Every page that defines it gives the same formula, and on a Dubai property that formula answers the wrong question twice. It puts costs on the wrong side of the division, because one of the statutory charges here falls on occupied nights rather than on the year. And it measures against zero, when the thing an owner gave up was never zero.

## The split that decides the answer

Break-even occupancy is fixed costs divided by what a fully occupied year leaves after the costs that scale. Everything turns on which costs go where, and the test is a single question: does this line stop when the unit is empty.

The same illustrative unit as [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/): AED 1,500,000, 900 sq ft, AED 105,000 a year in rent, service charge at AED 18 a foot. Figures are illustrative and the assumptions are stated, but the structure is not.

| Line | Amount | Stops when the unit is empty |
|---|---|---|
| Service charge | 16,200 | No |
| Maintenance reserve | 5,250 | No |
| Insurance and fixed costs | 1,500 | No |
| **Fixed** | **22,950** | |
| Management, at 5% of rent collected | 5,250 | Yes |

The service charge is the clearest case, and it is set by an instrument rather than by a landlord: Law No. (6) of 2019 puts the charge on the owner of the unit, which is why an empty flat in a serviced tower keeps billing. [Service charges and reserves](/playbooks/service-charge-and-reserves/) takes that apart properly.

So a fully let year collects AED 105,000, pays AED 5,250 in management, and has AED 99,750 to cover AED 22,950 of fixed cost. **Break-even occupancy is 23.01%.** The unit can sit empty for nine months of the year and still not cost its owner money.

That is the number for a property with no mortgage. It is also the number almost nobody actually has.

## What a mortgage does to it

A mortgage instalment is fixed cost in its entirety. It does not care whether a tenant is present, and it moves the ratio further than any other single line.

At the 75% loan to value this site's worked example assumes, the loan is AED 1,125,000, and the Central Bank sets the outer term: "The maximum tenor of the mortgage loan is 25 years". Over that term, at each rate:

| Mortgage rate | Annual instalment | Break-even occupancy |
|---|---|---|
| 4.0% | 71,258 | 94.45% |
| 4.5% | 75,037 | 98.24% |
| 5.0% | 78,920 | Never |
| 5.5% | 82,902 | Never |
| 6.0% | 86,981 | Never |

Read the bottom three rows carefully, because they are not a rounding. **At 5% the property cannot break even at any occupancy at all.** A unit let for all 365 days of the year, with no void, no bad tenant and no rent free period, still does not cover its own costs and its own instalment.

The reason is arithmetic rather than opinion. Fully let, after management, the unit leaves **AED 76,800** for a lender. An instalment larger than that cannot be paid out of the property, whatever the occupancy. **The highest mortgage rate this unit's own rent can carry at 75% loan to value is 4.72%.**

Above that rate the property is not an income asset that happens to be geared. It is a leveraged position on price with a monthly subsidy attached, and it is worth describing that way when deciding whether to keep it. Nothing here says the position is wrong. It says the position should be named accurately, and that the cash to fund it has to come from somewhere the property is not.

This is also the honest answer to a question [mortgage capacity](/playbooks/mortgage-capacity/) raises and does not close: the Central Bank's ratios ask whether the borrower can service the loan, not whether the property can. Those are different tests and they can disagree.

## The holiday home, and the benchmark nobody uses

Now the version of this question people actually search for, which is how many nights a Dubai holiday home has to sell before it is worth running.

[Short let versus long let](/playbooks/short-let-vs-long-let/) sets out the full cost stack and the instruments behind it. The relevant structure here is that the costs fall into three blocks, not two, and only one of them is recovered by occupancy.

| Block | Amount | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Operator and platform | 20% of gross | A share of what comes in |
| Tourism dirham and cleaning | 60 a night | Charged on nights sold |
| Fixed annual block | 55,300 | Owed whether or not anyone stays |

At the illustrative AED 750 a night, one more night sold brings in AED 750, of which AED 150 goes to the operator and AED 60 goes on the tourism dirham and the share of a clean. **Each night contributes AED 540**, and the fixed block of AED 55,300 is recovered at that rate.

Which gives the first break-even. AED 55,300 at AED 540 a night is **103 nights, or 28.2% occupancy**. Below that the unit loses money. Above it, the unit makes money.

And that is the number every competing page prints, and it is not the number that matters.

Because the alternative to a quiet holiday home is not an empty flat. It is the annual tenancy the owner gave up in order to run one, which on this unit nets **AED 78,550** with no nights to sell, no guests to manage and no forecast to believe. To beat a contract that pays AED 78,550, the short let has to recover the fixed block *and* that amount: AED 133,850 at AED 540 a night.

**That is 248 nights, or 67.9% occupancy.**

| Measured against | Nights | Occupancy |
|---|---|---|
| Zero, the usual answer | 103 | 28.2% |
| The annual tenancy it replaced | 248 | 67.9% |

**The gap is 145 nights.** Almost five months of the year, in which the holiday home is profitable on every page written about it and is nevertheless doing worse than a signed tenancy that took an afternoon to arrange. An owner running at 50% occupancy is well clear of break-even in the sense the word is normally used, and is losing roughly a month and a half of income against the thing they stopped doing.

## Where the statute actually sits, which is not where people look

The Dubai specific costs are the ones owners expect to be the obstacle. They are not, and the arithmetic says so precisely.

The holiday home permit under Executive Council Resolution No. (49) of 2014 is AED 300 per bedroom, capped at AED 1,200 a year. On this one bedroom that is **AED 300, which is 0.5% of the AED 55,300 fixed block.** Furnishing, amortised, is fifty times larger. The permit is not what makes this hard.

The tourism dirham matters structurally rather than in size. Executive Council Resolution No. (2) of 2014 sets it at AED 10 per occupied room per night, and the words "per occupied room per night" are the whole point: it is charged on nights sold, so it belongs with the operator's share and the cleaning, reducing what each night contributes. A page that files it beside the permit as an annual cost of compliance has put it on the wrong side of the division and will understate break-even occupancy for every unit that is busy.

At 248 nights the tourism dirham comes to AED 2,480. It is a real cost and it is not the reason the answer is 67.9%.

## The frontier, which is the part to keep

One nightly rate gives one answer, and an operator's forecast will not use the rate assumed here. So the useful form of this page is the pair: at each nightly rate, the occupancy that covers costs and the occupancy that beats the tenancy.

| Nightly rate | Nights to cover costs | Occupancy | Nights to beat the tenancy | Occupancy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | 163 | 44.7% | Never | Never |
| 600 | 132 | 36.2% | 319 | 87.4% |
| 700 | 111 | 30.4% | 268 | 73.4% |
| 750 | 103 | 28.2% | 248 | 67.9% |
| 800 | 97 | 26.6% | 232 | 63.6% |
| 900 | 84 | 23.0% | 203 | 55.6% |
| 1000 | 75 | 20.5% | 181 | 49.6% |

The top row is the one worth sitting with. **At AED 500 a night there is no occupancy in the year that beats the annual tenancy**, including 365 nights out of 365. Each night contributes AED 340 after the operator's share and the per night charges, and a full year of that does not reach AED 133,850. Below **AED 534 a night** the question of occupancy does not arise at all, and a forecast quoting a rate under that is answering a question the owner did not ask.

## What to do with somebody else's forecast

The one thing the pages that rank for this query have and this page does not is observed market data: real occupancy and real average daily rates, by building and by season. That data is worth having, and it is sold by firms that collect it. Nothing here substitutes for it.

What the frontier does is tell you what to do with it once you have it. Take the two numbers any operator or data provider gives you, the nightly rate and the occupancy, and find them in the table. If the pair sits below the fourth column, the forecast is describing a unit that would earn more on a twelve month contract, however profitable the forecast says it is. That is a question the forecast will not raise on its own, because the firm making it is not paid to.

Three follow-ups worth asking in the same conversation. Whether the quoted occupancy is nights sold or nights available after owner use, because the two differ by however long the owner intends to stay. Whether the quoted rate is before or after platform commission, since a 20% difference moves every row in that table. And whether the building permits short letting at all, which is the developer's or the owners' association's decision and not the operator's.

## Reading the answer

**Under 50%.** The property carries itself through a bad year. Typically unleveraged, or leveraged lightly against a modest service charge.

**50% to 70%.** Normal for a sensibly geared rental. A month or two of vacancy is absorbed by the margin rather than funded from elsewhere.

**70% to 85%.** Thin. A single difficult re-letting, or a service charge increase of the kind [rent increase caps](/playbooks/rent-increase-caps/) cannot offset because caps bind rent and not costs, moves the owner to funding the asset.

**Above 85%.** The property is an income asset in name only.

**No answer at all.** The rows above that read "Never" are not a failure of the model. They are the model working, and they are common on leveraged Dubai stock at current mortgage rates.

## The portfolio version

Run it across everything owned at once rather than unit by unit: fixed costs from every property against total rent at full occupancy, less the fees that scale. That number describes what a market wide soft patch does, which is the case where several units go quiet together rather than one going quiet alone.

It is also the number to have before selling anything, because an owner deciding which unit to release usually reaches for the lowest yield. The unit worth releasing is the one carrying the highest break-even occupancy, and those are frequently not the same property. [Selling well](/playbooks/selling-well/) covers what that exit costs once the decision is made.
