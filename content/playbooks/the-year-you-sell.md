There is a version of this decision that people make without noticing they made it. They decide to buy property in Dubai, they decide to move, and somewhere in the middle they sell the asset that funds it. The order those three things happen in is frequently accidental, and for anybody leaving the United Kingdom it is the largest single financial decision in the sequence.

So here is the answer first, in the one case this page can price. **Under paragraph 110 of Schedule 45 to the Finance Act 2013, a UK resident who leaves, sells an asset while abroad, and is UK resident again after an absence of five years or less is charged UK capital gains tax on that gain in the year of the return, which at the 2026 to 2027 higher rate of 24% is 95,280 pounds on a gain of 400,000 pounds.** Selling at a cleverer moment does not avoid it. Coming back later does.

That is not a general warning about tax. It is a published rule with four conditions, a date, and a number, and the rest of this page is those four conditions, that date and that number.

## The four conditions, in the statute's own words

The rule is [Finance Act 2013, Schedule 45, Part 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/29/schedule/45/part/4), which rewrote sections 10A and 10AA of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992. Paragraph 110(1) makes an individual "temporarily non-resident" where four things are true together.

| Paragraph | What it requires |
|---|---|
| 110(1)(a) | Sole UK residence for a residence period, called period A |
| 110(1)(b) | One or more periods without sole UK residence immediately after it |
| 110(1)(c) | Sole UK residence in 4 of the 7 tax years before the year of departure |
| 110(1)(d) | An absence of 5 years or less |

Two of those four are where the competing pages go wrong.

**The entry test is not residence.** The condition is that the individual has sole UK residence for a residence period, and paragraph 112(3) withholds that status from anybody who, at any time in the year, "falls to be regarded as resident in a country outside the UK for the purposes of double taxation arrangements". So the question at 110(1)(c) is whether at least 4 out of the 7 tax years immediately preceding the year of departure were years of sole UK residence, not years of UK residence. Somebody who spent four of those seven years treaty resident elsewhere was never inside this rule, and a page that prints the looser test will tell him he is.

**The clock is a statutory period, not a stay abroad.** Paragraph 113 defines it as the span between the end of period A and the start of the next residence period of sole UK residence, and paragraph 115 says the period of return "is the first residence period after period A for which the individual has sole UK residence". Paragraph 114 is equally literal about the other end: the year of departure "is the tax year consisting of or including period A". The charge bites where the temporary period of non-residence is 5 years or less, measured between those two points.

This matters in a way that a calendar does not capture. A single tax year of UK residence in the middle of an overseas spell closes one period and opens another, so somebody who thinks of himself as having been abroad for seven years may have two absences of three and four. A gain is outside the clawback only where the absence runs longer than five years, because paragraph 110(1)(d) catches a person where the temporary period of non-residence is 5 years or less, and that period is measured between residence periods rather than between addresses.

## The year the bill lands is the year you come home

Section 10A(2), as substituted by paragraph 119, does not tax the gain in the year of the disposal. It charges the taxpayer as if the gains were "accruing to the taxpayer in the period of return".

Section 10A(2) charges the taxpayer as if the gains were accruing to the taxpayer in the period of return, so a gain realised in the second year of a Dubai posting and followed by a return in the fourth is taxed in that fourth year at that year's rates.

That single piece of drafting is the reason the usual advice is the wrong way round. Every page on this subject tells a reader to think carefully about when to sell. Within an absence that ends inside the period, when he sells makes no difference to the charge at all: the same gain, realised in year one or in year four, is taxed in the year of return at the rates of the year of return. The variable that is actually load-bearing is the date of the return, and that is the one most people treat as a family decision rather than a tax one.

HMRC's own [Capital Gains Manual](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg26500) states the intention plainly: the provisions "broadly aim to charge any gains that accrue in the period when the individual was not UK resident to capital gains tax by treating them as accruing in the year or residence period in which the individual resumes UK residence".

## What coming home early costs

With the rule known, the bill is arithmetic. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates) sets the rate: a higher or additional rate taxpayer will "pay 24% on your gains from 6 April 2026". The annual exempt amount for 2026 to 2027 is 3,000 pounds. Nothing else is needed.

| Gain | Taxable after the allowance | Charge at 24% | Effective rate on the whole gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | 97,000 | 23,280 | 23.28% |
| 250,000 | 247,000 | 59,280 | 23.71% |
| 400,000 | 397,000 | 95,280 | 23.82% |
| 500,000 | 497,000 | 119,280 | 23.86% |
| 1,000,000 | 997,000 | 239,280 | 23.93% |
| 2,000,000 | 1,997,000 | 479,280 | 23.96% |

Read the last column rather than the third. Across every gain large enough to fund a Dubai purchase the effective rate sits between 23.28% and 23.96%, so the charge is very close to a flat 24% of the gain and the allowance is the only thing bending it. A reader who wanted to know whether this rule is a rounding error or a material sum now has the answer in a form he can apply to his own figure without a calculator: take 24% and stop.

Set that against the thing it is usually weighed against. The entire Dubai transaction stack on the illustrative purchase, every fee from the Land Department's four per cent down, costs 0.28 points of yield once, as [transaction cost drag](/playbooks/transaction-cost-drag/) sets out. This is a quarter of the gain, once, and it is decided by a date somebody picks for reasons that have nothing to do with it.

## The basic rate band cannot rescue it

There is one genuine variable, and it is worth pricing precisely so that it can then be set aside. Gains falling inside the basic rate band are charged at 18% rather than 24%, and the band is 37,700 pounds, which GOV.UK gives as the basic rate band for the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

Multiply it out. The gap between the two rates is six points, so the most the band can ever be worth on a capital gain is 37,700 times six per cent, which is **2,262 pounds**. That is the ceiling, available only to somebody with no other taxable income at all in the year of return.

On the 400,000 pound gain above, 2,262 pounds is **0.57 points** of effective rate. It is the one number on this page a reader cannot pin down without knowing his own income in the year he comes back, and it is too small to change any decision this page is about. Saying so is more useful than saying it depends.

## The asset you bought after you left is outside the rule

Section 10AA(1) is the carve-out, and it is the part of this rule that is actually good news for somebody buying in Dubai. The charge does not apply to a gain on a disposal where "the asset was acquired by the taxpayer in the temporary period of non-residence", subject to three further conditions in 10AA(1)(b) to (d) about assets received on no-gain-no-loss transfers and interests under settlements.

Section 10AA(1)(a) disapplies the charge where the asset was acquired by the taxpayer in the temporary period of non-residence, so the flat bought after arriving is outside it and the exposure runs to whatever was already held on the day of departure.

So the exposure is a question about the past, not the future. The crypto, the shares or the business sold to fund the purchase were almost certainly held on the day of departure, and those are what the rule reaches. The Dubai flat bought in year two of the posting is an asset acquired during the absence, and a gain on selling it is outside section 10A(2) altogether. The UAE, for its part, levies no personal income tax on it either.

That inverts the shape of the problem. The rule is not a reason to delay buying in Dubai. It is a reason to be careful about selling what you already own, and the care consists of one question: when do you intend to be UK resident again?

## The year of departure stays open until 2030

Section 10AA(5) suspends the ordinary assessment time limits. An assessment for the year of departure may be made at any time "before the end of the second anniversary of the 31 January next following the year of return".

Work that through for somebody who returns in the 2026 to 2027 tax year. The 31 January next following is 31 January 2028, and the second anniversary of that is **31 January 2030**. The year of departure, which may be four or five years earlier, remains assessable until then.

This is why a departure does not close the question, and why the records that prove a residence position are worth keeping far longer than feels necessary. [Residency and tax](/playbooks/residency-and-tax/) covers the four separate questions, residence, citizenship, source and situs, that are routinely collapsed into one, and [tax residency](/glossary/tax-residency/) is the term itself.

## UK land is a different charge on a different clock

One exclusion matters enough to state rather than imply. None of the above governs a disposal of UK property or land, which is chargeable to a non-resident under its own regime. HMRC is explicit that a person who is not resident in the UK "must report disposals of UK property or land even if you" have no tax to pay, have made a loss, or are already in Self Assessment, and the deadline is "60 days of selling the property if the completion date was on or after 27 October 2021".

So somebody selling a UK buy-to-let to fund a Dubai purchase has a reporting obligation on a two month clock that runs from completion, regardless of everything on this page. That charge is not priced here, and the two should not be confused: one is a rule about coming back, and the other is a rule about where the land is.

## A nil liability is not a nil obligation

A small but recurring error: owing nothing and having to file nothing are different states.

A jurisdiction being left behind commonly wants a return for the year of departure. A jurisdiction that charges nothing on a gain may still expect it declared. And the transaction at the other end has its own paper requirements, because a UAE property purchase funded from a virtual asset triggers a reporting obligation on the broker regardless of how the funds arrive, which is [proving the source of crypto funds](/playbooks/proving-the-source-of-crypto-funds/).

Those two files want the same underlying documents. Assembling them once, early, serves both.

## Where this page stops

It prices one rule in one country, and it does so because that rule is published, numbered and arithmetical. It does not price the equivalent in any other jurisdiction, and the equivalents differ enough that the UK figures above should not be read across to one. An Indian citizen's exposure, for instance, runs through a residence test rather than a clawback, and is set out on [residency and tax](/playbooks/residency-and-tax/).

Nor does it decide whether this charge is avoidable in a given case. Two jurisdictions, one asset and a set of dates is precisely the situation where a general framework stops being useful, and a split year on departure, a treaty position or the particular route by which an asset was acquired can each move the answer. What this page is for is making the question arrive early enough to be worth asking, with the size of it already known, so that the conversation with somebody qualified starts from a number rather than from scratch.

Relative to a disposal large enough to fund a property, that conversation is inexpensive. It is also the only step in this sequence that cannot be taken retrospectively.
