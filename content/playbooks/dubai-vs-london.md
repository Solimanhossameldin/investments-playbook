Most pages ranking for this question print the two gross yields side by side, Dubai at seven and London at five, and stop. The worked examples that go further usually charge a 3% additional property surcharge that was replaced by 5% in October 2024, and leave out the 2% non-resident surcharge entirely, which is the one that applies to a buyer who lives in Dubai. This page runs one London flat and one Dubai flat through everything the two governments set, and nothing else.

## The short answer

For someone who lives in Dubai, a London flat bought at the London average price keeps **2.42%** of what it cost after UK tax this year, and **2.36%** from April 2027. The Dubai flat on the [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page keeps **4.33%**, and no tax is charged on it. London's stamp duty alone is **£56,532, 10.20% of the price**, and it takes **3.83 years** of the rent you keep to earn it back.

**On the Office for National Statistics' average London rent of £2,338 a month for September 2026 and HM Land Registry's average London price of £554,430 for August 2026, the non-resident stamp duty on an average London flat is £56,532, or 10.20% of the price, and it takes 3.83 years of kept rent to earn back, which is why that flat nets 2.42% to a Dubai resident where an equivalent Dubai one bedroom nets 4.33% and is taxed at nothing.**

If you live in the UK instead, the gap between the two cities narrows from **1.91** points to **0.75**, because the UK taxes your Dubai rent too. Most of Dubai's lead is where the owner lives, not where the building is.

## The two flats

**London** is taken at the two official releases that actually print the figures, not at a brokerage's "typical" yield: an average London rent of **£2,338 a month** in September 2026, from the Office for National Statistics, and an average London price of **£554,430** in August 2026, from HM Land Registry's UK House Price Index. That is a gross yield of **5.06%**. The two series average different stocks of homes, the rented one and the sold one, and they carry different reference months, so treat 5.06% as the city's rough centre rather than a quote for a building.

A note on where that price comes from, because this page had it wrong until 30 October 2026. The ONS bulletin gives London's annual change and how far the average sits below its peak, but it never prints the level itself. This page previously used **£569,000** and dated it July 2026. That figure is the July 2025 peak: London's actual July 2026 average was £550,037, so the price here was overstated by 3.4%, and the stamp duty with it. The level now comes from the UK House Price Index summary, which states it outright.

**Dubai** is the illustrative one bedroom the net rental yield page prices from the Land Department's fee schedule: AED 1,500,000, AED 105,000 rent, a **7.00%** gross yield.

Then one deliberate choice. The London flat is given **exactly the same running cost ratio** as the Dubai one, vacancy, service charge, management, maintenance and insurance together taking 34.2% of the rent. London leasehold service charges, ground rent and letting fees are, if anything, heavier than that, so this flatters London. It also removes every line that is a matter of opinion, which leaves only the lines that are a matter of law.

## Getting in

In England the purchase tax is Stamp Duty Land Tax, and the buyer this site is written for pays two surcharges on top of the standard bands. The first applies "if buying a new residential property means you'll own more than one": 5 percentage points. The second: "You'll usually pay a 2% surcharge if you're buying a residential property in England or Northern Ireland" if you have not been in the UK for "at least 183 days (6 months) during the 12 months before your purchase". Both are added to every band, including the first £125,000 that would otherwise be taxed at nothing.

| Slice of the £554,430 price | Rate | Tax, £ |
|---|---|---|
| First 125,000 | 7% | 8,750 |
| Next 125,000 | 9% | 11,250 |
| Remaining 304,430 | 12% | 36,532 |
| **Total** | | **56,532** |

**£56,532, or 10.20% of the price.** A buyer who is UK resident and owns another property pays £45,443; the non-resident surcharge is the difference.

In Dubai the Land Department's registration fee is 4% and the whole acquisition stack, agency commission and trustee included, is **6.41%** of the price. London buyers do not normally pay an agent, which narrows the gap, and London's figure above leaves out conveyancing and survey, which widens it again. The statutory tax alone is **4% against 10.20%**.

## Holding it

UK rent is UK taxable income wherever the landlord lives. Under the Non-resident Landlord Scheme the letting agent or tenant will "deduct basic rate tax from your rent (after allowing for any expenses they've paid)", unless HMRC approves you to receive it gross, and either way "You need to declare your rental income in a Self Assessment tax return unless HMRC tells you not to".

From 6 April 2027 the rates on property income go up by two points: "22% at the property basic rate, 42% at the property higher rate, and 47% at the property additional rate for 2027 to 2028". The scheme's withholding rate moves with them.

Whether you get the personal allowance as a non-resident depends on your citizenship and the treaty, so both cases are shown. On the London flat's £18,471 of net operating income:

| Tax year and allowance | UK tax, £ | Kept, £ | Net yield |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 to 2027, basic rate, no allowance | 3,694 | 14,777 | 2.42% |
| 2026 to 2027, basic rate, with allowance | 1,180 | 17,291 | 2.83% |
| 2027 to 2028, property basic rate, no allowance | 4,064 | 14,407 | 2.36% |
| 2027 to 2028, property basic rate, with allowance | 1,298 | 17,173 | 2.81% |

The allowance assumes the flat is your only UK income. Any UK salary or pension uses it first, and then the rent is taxed from the first pound.

| Statutory purchase tax, % of price | 4.00% | 10.20% |

The London row uses this year's rates and no personal allowance. Dubai's entry cost is the whole stack; London's is the stamp duty alone.

## Leaving

Dubai charges no capital gains tax. The exit is the agency commission, which [selling well](/playbooks/selling-well/) prices.

A non-resident selling a UK home pays UK capital gains tax at 18% or 24% depending on where the gain falls against the income tax bands. "For the 2026 to 2027 tax year the allowance is £3,000." The stamp duty you paid is part of your cost, so it comes off the gain first. On £100,000 of gain after that, the tax is **£23,280** at 24% and **£17,460** at 18%.

Two procedural points catch non-residents. You must report the sale even if you "have no tax to pay on the disposal", and you must report and pay within "60 days of selling the property". Miss it and you may have to pay interest and a penalty.

## Where you live decides more than where you buy

Everything above is for an owner in Dubai. Run the same two flats for a UK higher rate taxpayer and both numbers move. The London flat now pays the 5% surcharge but not the non-resident one, £45,443, and its profit is taxed at 40%. The Dubai flat's rent is taxed in the UK too, and the double tax treaty gives no relief because there is no UAE tax to credit. The [tax on Dubai rental income](/playbooks/residency-and-tax/) page works that through.

| Owner lives in | Dubai flat | London flat | Dubai's lead, points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dubai | 4.33% | 2.42% | 1.91 |
| UK, higher rate | 2.60% | 1.85% | 0.75 |

Net yield after entry costs and tax, this year's rates. Dubai wins in both rows, but for the UK resident more than half of the advantage has gone. The zero tax that makes the Dubai number is a property of the owner's residence, and a buyer who has not left the UK has not got it.

## So what is London for

Not income. Three things, and they are real.

**Currency.** The dirham is pegged to the dollar. A portfolio held entirely in dollar assets is a single bet, and for a family whose children may study or settle in the UK, sterling assets are a liability match rather than a speculation. The [currency risk](/playbooks/currency-risk-and-the-peg/) page covers the peg.

**Depth.** Title, planning law and a rental market with centuries of case law behind it. Dubai's framework is good and improving, and it is decades old.

**Growth, if you believe in it.** It is the argument most London buyers are actually making, and it is the one this page cannot price. The ONS reports London prices down 2.2% in the twelve months to August 2026, £15,000 below their July 2025 peak of £569,000 and £8,000 above their March 2026 low.

The composition under that headline matters more than the headline, for anyone buying the unit this comparison buys. **In the twelve months to August 2026 the average price of a London flat fell 5.0%, while average prices for London houses rose over the same period, and flats are approximately half of London sales, according to the Office for National Statistics.** The city average is being held up by the half of the market an overseas investor does not usually buy, and dragged down by the half he does. Dubai's own record, including the 85 months it took to regain its 2015 peak, is on the [price index](/dubai-price-index/) page. For a Dubai resident, London prices have to outgrow Dubai's by about two points a year, and more once its capital gains tax is paid, just to make up the income the London flat gives up.

None of those is a yield argument. If the case for London is diversification, size it as diversification. If the case is income, the tables above are the case against. The same test on [Singapore](/playbooks/dubai-vs-singapore/) reaches the same answer more bluntly, and the [net rental yield calculator](/calculators/net-rental-yield/) runs the Dubai side on your own numbers.
