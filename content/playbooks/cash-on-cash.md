On the illustrative one bedroom this site uses throughout, **AED 1,500,000**, 900 square feet, let at AED 105,000 a year, with **AED 69,127** of net operating income, a 75% loan to value mortgage over the 25 year maximum tenor turns the flat's cash flow negative above a mortgage rate of **3.71%**. The rate at which borrowing stops improving the return on your own money is **4.30%**. Those are two different numbers, 0.59 points apart, and the gap between them is the principal.

Net yield tells you what the asset earns. Cash on cash tells you what reaches your bank account, which is a different question the moment you borrow, and over a band of mortgage rates the two give opposite answers.

## Two break-evens, and the band between them

[Mortgage versus cash](/playbooks/mortgage-vs-cash/) nets interest against income, deliberately: a repayment instalment also returns capital, which is saving rather than cost, and counting it as a cost would overstate the drag. That is the right treatment for a question about return, and it produces the 4.30%.

It is the wrong treatment for a question about cash. The lender is owed the whole instalment whether or not the principal inside it is notionally your own saving, and the current account does not know the difference. Netting the full debt service against net operating income produces a second, lower threshold, and between the two:

| At a mortgage rate of | Does borrowing raise the return on your money? | Does the flat pay you? |
|---|---|---|
| Below 3.71% | Yes | Yes |
| **3.71% to 4.30%** | **Yes** | **No** |
| Above 4.30% | No | No |

The middle row is the one no competing page prints, because printing it requires computing both thresholds rather than one. At the 4.30% break-even itself the flat runs a cash flow of **−AED 4,386** a year, a cash on cash return of **−0.92%**, against the **4.33%** the same flat returns bought outright. A buyer at that rate has been shown, correctly, that the loan improves their return, and will fund the shortfall from salary every month for as long as they hold it.

## The worked example, rate by rate

The buyer puts in **AED 478,503** of their own money: AED 375,000 of deposit, the AED 96,220 acquisition stack itemised line by instrument on [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/), and the AED 7,283 that registering the mortgage costs in its own fee, trustee and title deed. None of the last two blocks can be borrowed, for the reason worked through on [how much you can borrow](/playbooks/mortgage-capacity/): the loan to value cap is a percentage of the value of the property, and a fee is not the property.

| Mortgage rate | Monthly instalment | Annual debt service | Year-one cash flow | Cash on cash return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.00% | 5,335 | 64,019 | 5,108 | 1.07% |
| 3.50% | 5,632 | 67,584 | 1,543 | 0.32% |
| 4.00% | 5,938 | 71,258 | −2,131 | −0.45% |
| 4.50% | 6,253 | 75,037 | −5,910 | −1.24% |
| 5.00% | 6,577 | 78,920 | −9,793 | −2.05% |
| 5.50% | 6,908 | 82,902 | −13,775 | −2.88% |
| 6.00% | 7,248 | 86,981 | −17,854 | −3.73% |

Every row above 3.71% is negative, and 3.71% is below every mortgage rate in the table bar the first two. On this flat, at this service charge, leverage at the regulatory maximum does not produce income. It produces a monthly bill and a claim on the price.

## What the negative number is not

It is not a loss. Part of each instalment repays principal, and that part is equity accumulating in an asset the buyer owns.

| At 4.50% | Amount |
|---|---|
| Year-one interest | 50,115 |
| Year-one principal | 24,922 |
| Year-one cash flow | −5,910 |
| Cash flow plus principal | 19,012 |

So the honest statement is that the flat takes AED 5,910 out of the buyer's pocket and puts AED 24,922 into their equity, and whether that is a good trade depends on whether they have the AED 5,910 to spare every year without selling something. That is a liquidity question, not a return question, and it is the question the ratio exists to ask. A buyer who cannot fund the gap is a forced seller on a timetable set by their salary rather than by the market.

## Borrowing more moves one break-even and not the other

| Loan to value | Loan | Your own money | Return break-even | Cash-flow break-even |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 750,000 | 852,565 | 4.29% | 7.94% |
| 60% | 900,000 | 702,940 | 4.30% | 5.93% |
| 70% | 1,050,000 | 553,315 | 4.30% | 4.37% |
| 75% | 1,125,000 | 478,503 | 4.30% | 3.71% |
| 80% | 1,200,000 | 403,690 | 4.30% | 3.11% |

The two columns could not behave less alike. The return break-even moves **one basis point** across a thirty point swing in the loan. The cash-flow break-even moves **4.83 points** across the same bands.

The mechanism is in the shape of each comparison. The return break-even sets a yield against a rate, and both sides scale with the loan, so the loan very nearly cancels out. The cash-flow break-even sets a fixed income against a payment that scales with the loan, and nothing cancels at all: the rent is what the flat produces, and it does not care how the purchase was financed.

What follows is the opposite of how leverage is usually described. Borrowing more does not buy a more forgiving threshold — the rate at which borrowing stops paying is the same 4.30% at every band a completed purchase can use. It collapses the rate at which the flat stops paying for itself, from 7.94% at half the price to 3.11% at four fifths of it. A buyer at 50% loan to value can absorb a mortgage at almost eight per cent and still be paid by the flat. The same buyer at 80% cannot absorb one above three.

## Check occupancy before you check either

Both thresholds assume the flat is let for the year at the rent modelled. The share of the year it must be let simply to stand still is a separate calculation, it is usually the more urgent one, and it has its own page in [break-even occupancy](/playbooks/break-even-occupancy/). Two flats with identical cash on cash returns can carry very different break-even occupancy, and that difference is the actual risk being taken.

The input that moves all of this furthest is neither the rate nor the loan. It is the service charge, which is set annually by the owners association, is published building by building, and is the one figure a listing never carries — which is why it has [a page and a lookup](/playbooks/service-charge-and-reserves/) of its own.
