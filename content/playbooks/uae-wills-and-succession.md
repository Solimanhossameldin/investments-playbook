## The answer, before the setup

A non-Muslim who dies owning a Dubai flat and leaves no registered will is not subject to Sharia forced heirship. That stopped being the default on 1 February 2023. The instrument is [Federal Decree-Law No. (41) of 2022](https://uaelegislation.gov.ae/en/legislations/1586), and Article 11(2) opens with a sentence short enough to quote whole: **half of the inheritance shall devolve to the husband or wife, and the other half shall be distributed equally among the children with no differentiation between male and female**.

On the illustrative one bedroom this site uses throughout, AED 1,500,000, the same flat as the [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page, that is arithmetic rather than interpretation.

| Surviving family | Spouse, AED | Each child, AED |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse and 1 child | 750,000 | 750,000 |
| Spouse and 2 children | 750,000 | 375,000 |
| Spouse and 3 children | 750,000 | 250,000 |
| Spouse and 4 children | 750,000 | 187,500 |

Article 4(2) of the same Decree-Law says it in terms: **Inheritance shall be equally distributed between men and women in accordance with the provisions of this Decree-Law**. A daughter and a son take the same figure out of the same flat, and the spouse's half does not move with the number of children.

## Where the sentence stops

Now read the rest of Article 11(2), which is one continuous passage: **If the deceased has no children, the legacy shall devolve to the parents of the deceased if they are alive equally or half of the legacy shall devolve to either of the parents in the case the other parent has died, while the other half shall devolve to the brother and sisters thereof. In absence of such situations all the legacy shall devolve to either parent in absence of the testator's other parent, spouse, children or brothers and sisters. In the event of absence of both parents, the entire legacy shall devolve into the testator's brothers and sisters where it shall be divided on equal basis without differentiation between males and females**.

**It never says what the spouse gets.** The spouse's half appears in the sentence about children. No sentence in the Article fixes a spouse's share where there are none.

That is not a quibble, and the evidence it is not a quibble is that the pages currently answering this query disagree about it three ways, in print, today. Dubizzle reads the spouse as keeping the half the first sentence gives, with the parents dividing the other half. START's own FAQ says that if there are no children, the spouse takes the full estate. And the passage as drafted devolves the legacy on the parents without mentioning a spouse at all.

On the same AED 1,500,000 flat, those three readings pay a childless spouse:

| Reading | What the spouse receives, AED |
|---|---|
| The spouse keeps the half the first sentence gives | 750,000 |
| The legacy devolves on the parents as drafted | 0 |
| The spouse takes the whole estate | 1,500,000 |

The distance between the best and the worst reading is AED 1,500,000, the entire unit. A married owner with no children who leaves no will is not accepting a known default. He is leaving the whole flat to an argument.

That is the strongest reason on this page to register something, and it is a better reason than any the brokerage pages give, because it survives disagreement about everything else.

## The clauses that are determinate

Where the Article does fix a split it fixes it cleanly, and these are the cases where doing nothing has a knowable result.

| Case | Who takes what, on AED 1,500,000 |
|---|---|
| No children, one parent has died | Surviving parent 750,000, brothers and sisters divide 750,000 |
| No children, both parents have died | Brothers and sisters divide 1,500,000, equally |

Note what the second row means for a childless unmarried owner with two siblings: AED 750,000 each, with no step required from anyone, and nothing a partner of twenty years can do about it.

## The election belongs to the heirs, not to you

Article 11(3) is the provision nobody quotes, and it reverses the intuition most owners have: **any of the foreigner's heirs may request the application of the law applicable to the estate in accordance with the provisions stipulated in the Civil Code, unless there is a registered will to the contrary**.

Read it twice. The request is the heirs' to make, not the owner's, any one of them, after the owner is dead. And a registered will to the contrary switches it off.

So a will does two separate jobs. It directs the assets, which every page says. It also removes a decision your survivors would otherwise be able to take against each other at the worst moment of their lives. The second job is the one no page on this query mentions.

Article 1(1) sets the frame: the Decree-Law applies to **non-Muslims who are national citizens of the United Arab Emirates, and to non-Muslim foreigners residing in the state**, **unless any of them adheres to the application of the law of their home country**. Article 1(3) limits it to incidents after commencement, since **The provisions of this Decree-Law shall apply to all incidents that occur after the entry into force of these provisions**, and Article 18 fixes that date: the Decree-Law **shall enter into force as of February 1, 2023**.

## The one asset a home country will does not reach

A will drafted in London or Mumbai may well govern the house there. The position on a Dubai flat is different, and the governing words are in the Civil Transactions Law, Article 17(5): **The law of the United Arab Emirates shall apply to wills made by aliens disposing of their real property located in the State**.

A caveat on that citation, stated plainly. The only English text of Article 17 reachable from here is an unofficial translation, and the official portal entry for the Civil Transactions Law was not located. So this claim is sourced more weakly than everything above it, and it is the one claim on this page a reader should put to a lawyer rather than take from a website. It also happens to be a point on which the ranking pages openly contradict each other: START says a German testament cannot validly direct a Dubai apartment, while EGSH says a non-Muslim family's Dubai real estate may be handled under their national law.

What is not in doubt is the freedom in the other direction. Article 11(1): **The testator shall have the right to leave a will with the entire property he owns in the State in favor of anybody he wants**. Article 13(1) requires that wills **shall be recorded in the register prepared for this purpose**. The DIFC Courts operate such a register, established by a 2014 resolution **regulating inheritance, wills and probate for non-Muslims**.

One narrow provision worth knowing if you are about to marry in the UAE, from Article 13(2): **The married couple may fill out a will registration form during the signing of the marriage contract to indicate how the property will be distributed in the event of the death of either of them**.

## What certainty costs, against what the flat already cost you

The DIFC Courts publish a fee for each will type. Put it next to the one fee every Dubai owner has certainly paid, the 4% the Land Department charged to register the purchase, which on AED 1,500,000 was AED 60,000 and which is set out on the [cost of buying](/playbooks/cost-of-buying-in-dubai/) page.

| Will type | Single, AED | Mirror, AED | Single, as a share of the AED 60,000 transfer fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full Will | 10,000 | 15,000 | 16.7% |
| Property Will | 7,500 | 10,000 | 12.5% |
| Guardianship Will | 5,000 | 7,500 | 8.3% |
| Financial Assets Will | 5,000 | 7,500 | 8.3% |
| Business Owners Will | 5,000 | 7,500 | 8.3% |

A Property Will covering shares in up to five real estate properties within the UAE costs one eighth of what you paid the Land Department to put the first one in your name, and 0.5% of the unit. That is the whole trade. Whatever a reader concludes about the childless spouse question, the cost of taking it off the table is a rounding error against the transaction that created the asset.

These are the figures as published on the [DIFC Courts fees page](https://www.difccourts.ae/about/fees), which carries no effective date on its face. Confirm the number at booking.

## The other half of the problem, which a will does not fix

A UAE will governs where your UAE assets go. It does nothing about the [US estate tax](/glossary/us-estate-tax/) exposure created by holding US shares or US domiciled funds, which is a tax on the asset rather than a question of distribution, and which starts above a sixty thousand dollar exemption for a non-resident non-citizen. The [fund domicile](/playbooks/fund-domicile/) framework covers that side, and [situs](/glossary/situs/) is the concept that decides which rules reach which asset.

You can therefore have a perfectly drafted will directing assets to your family, and a tax bill on those same assets that the will does nothing to prevent.

## What to actually do

**Establish which assets are situated where.** UAE property and UAE accounts are UAE assets. A fund is situated where it is domiciled, not where your broker is.

**If you are married with no children, treat this as the urgent case.** The arithmetic above is the reason. It is the one family shape where the default is not a default.

**Register a will for the UAE assets**, sized to what you own. If there are minor children, the guardianship provision alone justifies it.

**Check the foreign wills still work.** Multiple wills across jurisdictions can accidentally revoke one another if they are not drafted to sit alongside each other.

**Review after every material change.** Marriage, divorce, a birth, a new property, a change of residency.

If you hold the flat through a company rather than in your own name, the succession question changes shape and becomes a question about shares. The [company or own name](/playbooks/corporate-vs-personal-ownership/) page sets out that threshold, and [residency and tax](/playbooks/residency-and-tax/) covers where you are resident for tax while you hold it.

## The honest caveat

This is a framework page, not legal advice, and succession is one of the areas where general information is least adequate. What this page adds over the alternatives is that it quotes the instrument instead of summarising it, names the one citation it is least sure of, and does the arithmetic. What it cannot do is tell you which reading of Article 11(2) a Dubai court would reach on the day, and anyone who tells you that in a blog post is guessing in a register that sounds like certainty.

What a page like this can usefully do is make sure the question gets asked, because the common failure is not choosing the wrong structure. It is never realising there was a choice.
