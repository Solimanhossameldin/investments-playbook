A management fee is the only number in a Dubai landlord's annual accounts that nobody sets. The Land Department publishes the tenancy registration to the fil, [Law No. (6) of 2019](/dubai-property-law/) forbids the company managing the building from collecting a service charge **without first obtaining the relevant approval of RERA**, and the Central Bank caps what a lender may charge for a liability letter. The fee charged to manage the flat itself is capped by nothing and published nowhere.

So the question worth asking is not what the fee is. It is what the fee demands, and that has an answer. A fee charged as a share of rent can be recovered from exactly two places: rent that would otherwise not have been collected, and rent that would otherwise not have been charged. Nothing else on a long let is in a manager's gift. Both have closed forms, and the first has a ceiling.

**On the illustrative one bedroom the vacancy route is shut above a fee of 7.69%, because that is the flat's own vacancy rate and no manager can remove more vacancy than the flat has.** Below it the fee is recoverable but not cheaply: a five per cent fee requires the manager to take 17.73 days out of a 28.08 day allowance, which is 63.16% of all the vacancy there is, simply to match what the owner would have netted doing it themselves.

## The one fee in the stack that nobody sets

Every other line on this site's property pages is traceable to an instrument. The registration fee is a rate in a Schedule. The rent increase is a band in a Decree. The early settlement fee is a row in an Appendix. The service charge is a budget RERA has to approve, and Law No. (6) of 2019 says so in terms.

The management fee is not like that, and the contrast is worth stating precisely rather than in general. The same firm, licensed under the same regime, managing the building the flat sits in, may not collect a fil of service charge without RERA's approval of the budget it comes from under Law No. (6) of 2019, while the fee it charges to manage the flat inside that building needs no approval from anyone. One of those numbers is public to the dirham on the Land Department's own index; the other is whatever is written on the proposal.

No Dubai instrument publishes a maximum management fee and this site holds no dataset of what firms actually charge, so no rate is estimated anywhere on this page: the rate is the one on the proposal in front of you, and the test below says whether it clears the 7.69% ceiling this flat sets. Inventing a band and attributing it to nothing would be the same error as the guides this page exists to replace.

## What the fee costs where you already measure it

The [net rental yield](/playbooks/net-rental-yield/) page prices this flat properly: the 900 square foot one bedroom at AED 1,500,000, letting for AED 105,000 a year, with a four week vacancy allowance and the approved service charge on it. Management sits in that stack as a line of AED 4,846, being five per cent of the rent actually collected rather than of the rent hoped for.

Taking the management line out moves net yield on the whole outlay from 4.33% to 4.63%, a gap of 0.30 points, which is 6.48% of the entire net yield. That is the cost of the fee stated in the unit a buyer compares properties in, and no competing page on this query states it in any unit at all.

## The first place a fee can come from: vacancy it removes

The fee is charged on rent collected, and vacancy is the rent not collected, so the two are fighting over the same money. An owner who self-manages and leaves the flat empty for four weeks collects AED 96,923. A manager who never leaves it empty for a day collects the full AED 105,000 and then takes a share of it.

Setting those two outcomes equal gives the reduction in vacancy that exactly pays for the fee, and it is a closed form: `(1 - v) x f / (1 - f)`, where `v` is the vacancy allowance and `f` the fee rate. The rows below are a span wide enough to contain whatever rate a proposal arrives at; none of them is a claim about what the market charges.

| Fee on rent collected | Vacancy it must remove, days | In weeks | Share of the whole allowance | Recoverable from vacancy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 17.73 | 2.53 | 63.16% | yes |
| 6% | 21.51 | 3.06 | 76.60% | yes |
| 7% | 25.36 | 3.61 | 90.32% | yes |
| 8% | 29.30 | 4.17 | 104.35% | no |
| 10% | 37.44 | 5.33 | 133.33% | no |

Read the last column before the others. At eight per cent the fee requires the manager to remove more vacancy than the flat is assumed to have, which is not a hard target but an impossible one: even a manager who lets the flat for every single day of the year hands the owner less than self-management did. The route closes at a fee rate of **7.69%**, and that figure is not asserted here, the module solves for it by bisection on the cut and it lands on the vacancy rate itself, which is the proof of the identity rather than a restatement of it.

That generalises, and it is the one sentence worth carrying off this page: **a management fee can only be recovered from vacancy while the fee rate is lower than the vacancy rate it is being hired to remove.** A flat that sits empty for two weeks a year supports a fee of under 3.85%. A flat that sits empty for eight supports one of under 15.4%. The worse the flat lets, the more a manager can justify charging, which is the opposite of how the quote is usually framed.

## The second place: rent it raises

The other route holds vacancy where it is and asks the manager to achieve a higher rent. The uplift needed is `f / (1 - f)`, slightly more than the fee itself because the fee is charged on the higher rent too.

On a renewal that uplift is not a market question. It is a legal one, because [Decree No. (43) of 2013](/playbooks/rent-increase-caps/) permits an increase only where the sitting rent is far enough below the index average, and permits none at all where the rent is **up to ten percent (10%) less than the average rental value**.

| Fee on rent collected | Rent uplift it needs | Lowest Decree 43 band that permits it |
|---|---|---|
| 5% | 5.26% | 21% to 30% below the index, which permits 10% |
| 6% | 6.38% | 21% to 30% below the index, which permits 10% |
| 7% | 7.53% | 21% to 30% below the index, which permits 10% |
| 8% | 8.70% | 21% to 30% below the index, which permits 10% |
| 10% | 11.11% | 31% to 40% below the index, which permits 15% |

The 11% to 20% band is missing from that table, and its absence is the finding: it permits a five per cent increase, and a five per cent fee needs 5.26%, so the smallest fee in the ladder is already too large for the second band to cover. **To recover even a five per cent fee from a sitting tenant's rent, the rent has to be at least 21% below the Smart Rental Index**, which is to say the owner must already have been under-letting by a fifth, and the manager is collecting a fee for fixing a problem the index would have shown for free.

Where the rent is correctly priced against the index, the rent route is closed by the Decree and the whole fee has to come out of vacancy, which puts the reader back in the table above with the ceiling in it.

## The one line here that is published to the dirham

Registering the tenancy is the part of this job with a public price, and it is worth knowing because it is often quoted back to an owner as an administrative charge. The Land Department's tenancy service publishes **AED 100 for registering a tenancy contract**, an AED 10 knowledge fee, an AED 10 innovation fee, and a service partner fee of AED 55 through the app or AED 95 at a trustee centre, with VAT falling on the partner's share and not on the Department's own lines.

Registering the tenancy comes to AED 177.75 through the app and AED 219.75 at a trustee centre, built from the Land Department's published lines rather than rounded to the figure the guides print. It is 0.17% of the rent on this flat. A management proposal is free to charge more than that for doing it, and several do; what it cannot do is describe the number as a government fee.

## What this does not settle

The ladder above is a long let, and it does not transfer to a short let. There the fee is charged on gross booking revenue, the revenue is a different and larger number, and the vacancy it is fighting is measured in nights rather than weeks, [short let against long let](/playbooks/short-let-vs-long-let/) does that comparison with the right base, and [break-even occupancy](/playbooks/break-even-occupancy/) gives the nights the flat has to fill before it earns anything at all.

Nor does this price the reasons an owner might pay a fee it cannot recover. Time has a value, so does distance from the property, so does not being the person a tenant calls at eleven at night, and so does having somebody with standing at the [Rental Disputes Centre](/playbooks/rent-increase-caps/) when a tenancy goes wrong. This site holds no dataset for any of them and does not pretend to. The arithmetic here settles one thing only, and it settles it completely: whether the fee can come back out of the rent. If the rate on the proposal is above the vacancy rate on the flat, the answer is no, and the conversation is about what else the fee is buying.
